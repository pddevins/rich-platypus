Junior, intermediate, senior, principal. We talk about these as levels, as though
there were a floor you arrive on and then stand around. Nobody has ever
experienced them that way. They're bridges, and you cross them one plank at a
time, usually without noticing which crossing you're in the middle of until
you're most of the way over.

<!--more-->

I came back from time off today — a birthday spent deliberately not thinking
about work — and the first thing on my mind was a developer I've been mentoring.
Mid-career. Genuinely good. Stuck, in the specific way that people get stuck when
they've cleared every obvious bar and nobody has told them what the next one is.

The conversation I used to have in that situation was about requirements. Here is
what senior means at this company, here are the gaps, close them. It isn't wrong,
exactly. It's just answering a question about identity with a checklist, and the
checklist never satisfies anyone, because the thing they actually want to know is
whether they're becoming someone they'd want to be.

So I stopped opening with the ladder. What I ask now is narrower and more
uncomfortable: which parts of last quarter did you want to keep doing after the
ticket closed? Not which parts you were good at — good is cheap, most competent
people are good at things they find tedious. Which parts left you with more energy
at the end than you had at the start.

The answers cluster in ways I didn't expect when I started asking. Some people
light up describing infrastructure, the quiet satisfaction of a system that stops
needing them. Some come alive in front of a client, translating between what
someone asked for and what they meant. Some want the architecture — the shape of
the thing, the decisions that are expensive to reverse. Some just want to write
code that's better than the code they wrote last year, and are slightly
embarrassed to admit that's enough. Some want their work to be public, in the
open, useful to strangers.

This one landed on architecture, which I'd half expected and had been careful not to
suggest. What they wanted was the shape of the thing — the decisions that are
expensive to reverse, the part where you're choosing what the system will find easy
and what it will find hard for the next four years.

Once you know that, the mentorship stops being abstract. You're no longer helping
someone become senior in general. You're helping someone energized by architecture
get more architecture, sooner, on something real, with enough cover that the first
expensive mistake doesn't end the experiment. Concretely: they own a design decision on
real work, and I review it before it ships rather than after. That's a plan.
"Demonstrate technical leadership" is not a plan.

This is also where I think the bridge metaphor earns its keep, because it changes
what a project is for. Every feature is a chance to try one thing you haven't
done or to sharpen one thing you've stopped thinking about. Most of the crossing
happens in the second category — the parts you take for granted, done again with
attention. You don't cross by taking on something enormous and heroic. You cross
by noticing that the way you've always handled a migration is a habit rather than
a decision, and deciding on purpose this time.

I want to take the obvious objection seriously, because I've been on the receiving
end of it. The external indicators are real. Titles gate access to rooms where
decisions get made. Salary bands are not a metaphor. Telling someone to focus on
what energizes them, while a promotion cycle is deciding whether they can afford a
house, is a way of asking them to absorb a cost you're not paying. Anyone who has
mentored people without acknowledging that has been giving advice that only works
for people with a financial cushion.

So both things have to be handled. Get the person paid and promoted — that's the
job, and it's on me, not them. But don't confuse it with the other work. The
promotion is a lagging indicator of a case someone else built. What I'm trying to
help with is the part that doesn't show up in the packet: whether five years from
now they're doing work that still interests them, or whether they optimized
successfully toward a version of themselves they don't much like.

The developers I've watched navigate this best weren't the ones who climbed
fastest. They were the ones who could tell you, specifically and without
hedging, which part of the craft they were in it for — and who then arranged
their next three projects around getting more of it. Sometimes that meant a
smaller client. Sometimes it meant a title that looked sideways on paper.

Privately, I think of the job as riding shotgun rather than driving. I can read the
map, say what's coming, and mention when I think a turn is about to be missed. But
it's their route, and the destination isn't a level. It's a morning some years from now
when the work in front of them is work they'd choose.
